# Reference results

All figures come from `analysis/reference_sensitivities.csv`, regenerated by

```powershell
uv run python scripts/verify_reference.py
uv run python scripts/run_reference_sensitivities.py
```

The asset is a hypothetical 100 MW / 200 MWh battery with 87% round-trip
efficiency at the Hornsdale 275 kV connection point in SA1. Every figure is
wholesale-energy only.

Read [Limitations](#limitations) before quoting any number here.

## Reference period

`(2022-11-01 00:00, 2022-12-01 00:00]` AEST — 8,640 five-minute observations,
30 committed windows.

November 2022 was chosen deliberately as the hardest month of FY2022-23 rather
than a representative one. 38.0% of its intervals are negative-priced, and 18
and 20 November are negative in all 288 intervals. A month that did not contain
those days would leave the exclusivity reduction and negative-price charging
effectively untested.

## Reading these scenarios

**Degradation cost drives both revenue and tractability.** It is the model's
only cycling penalty, so it sets how hard the battery works: 100.9 equivalent
full cycles at A$10/MWh against 54.6 at A$50/MWh. It also sets how many
exclusivity binaries survive the reduction — 1,988 at A$10/MWh against 170 at
A$50/MWh — which is why `degradation_10` takes ten times longer than every
other scenario. Extrapolating that trend is why A$0/MWh is out of scope; see
[VALIDATION.md](VALIDATION.md#solver-tolerance-and-where-exclusivity-binds).

**Endpoint effects are small here.** Starting at the 100 MWh midpoint adds only
A$549 over the headline, because the extra 90 MWh of unpriced opening inventory
is worth little against a month this volatile. Both cases end at the 10 MWh
floor.

**The salvage case now binds.** At A$400/MWh — above the realised spread — the
look-ahead salvage term holds 190 MWh at the end of the run instead of
depleting to the floor, and costs A$19,627 of cash revenue to do it. An earlier
A$100/MWh variant was dropped because it sat below the realised spread, never
changed the committed dispatch, and so tested nothing.

## Limitations

This is the complete list of modelling limitations. Everything else in this
repository that mentions scope or caveats points here rather than repeating a
partial version.

### Scope of revenue

- Wholesale-energy arbitrage only. FCAS, caps, contracts, network support,
  compensation and availability outages are excluded. FCAS is therefore exactly
  0% of *modelled* revenue, not an estimate of its share for a real battery.
- These figures are a lower bound on what a real battery at this connection
  point would earn, and are not comparable to published total battery revenue.

### Market behaviour

- Revenue is settled at the regional reference price with dated marginal loss
  factors. There is no market-impact model: the battery is a price taker, which
  overstates achievable revenue for an asset of this size in a region this
  small.
- No bidding behaviour is modelled. Dispatch is chosen directly rather than
  offered into a market that clears.

### Foresight

- Dispatch is perfect-foresight within each 48-hour window, of which the first
  24 hours are committed. That is materially easier than dispatching against a
  real forecast, and is not the same as annual perfect foresight either.

### Period

- One month is not a distribution, and November 2022 was chosen as an extreme
  rather than a typical month. Annualising any figure here would be misleading,
  which is why no per-MW-year number is quoted.
- A zero-degradation case is out of scope for tractability reasons; see
  [VALIDATION.md](VALIDATION.md#why-a0mwh-is-out-of-scope).

### Asset

- Degradation is a flat per-MWh charge, not a state-of-health model. It does not
  depend on depth of discharge, temperature, rate or age.
- No ramp constraint is imposed, because a grid battery can reverse within a
  five-minute interval.
- The asset is hypothetical. It is not the Hornsdale Power Reserve, and only the
  connection point and its published loss factors are real.
